# Most shooting stars are smaller than a grain of sand
- Question: what is a shooting star really, and why does a grain of dust glow so bright?
- Length: ~38 s · Language: en · Voice: chatterbox default, block mode, pitch −1 · Music: wonder
- Palette: night · Captions: punch

- Loop: "…Look up, and remember…" → "Most shooting stars are smaller than a grain of sand."

## Facts
1. The vast majority of meteors are only the size of sand grains; visible Leonids are 1 mm–1 cm — Sky & Telescope https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/observing-news/leonid-meteor-shower-to-the-rescue/
2. Leonids come from comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle (3.6 km, 33-year orbit); Earth crosses its debris every November — Space.com https://www.space.com/35-top-10-leonid-meteor-shower-facts.html ; NASA https://science.nasa.gov/?p=108062
3. Leonid entry speed 69.7 km/s (≈ 250,000 km/h) — NASA (above); computed 69.7 × 3600 = 250,920 km/h
4. Rifle bullet ≈ 0.9–1 km/s → ≈ 70× (phrased "about 70 times faster") — computed, rounded
5. Light comes from air compressed in front of the meteor heating up, not from rubbing/friction; heated to more than 3,000 °F (≈ 1,650 °C) — Space.com (above)
6. Burn-up in the mesosphere, 80–120 km up ("about 100 km") — Sky & Telescope (above)
7. 1966: thousands of meteors per minute for ~15 minutes on the morning of 17 Nov — EarthSky https://earthsky.org/astronomy-essentials/everything-you-need-to-know-leonid-meteor-shower/
8. 2026 peak: night of 17–18 Nov, ~10–15/h under dark skies, not a storm year — EarthSky; Star Walk https://starwalk.space/en/news/leonid-meteor-shower ; moon phase sources conflict → not mentioned
